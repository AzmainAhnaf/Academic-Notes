## 1. Determination of the Value of Acceleration Due to Gravity(g) by Means of A Compound Pendulum
![[Pasted image 20250822002203.png]]

Compound pendulum is a rigid body of any shape free to oscillate about a horizontal axis. The center of gravity at distance $l$ from $A$ is denoted by $G$. The pendulum experiences a restoring angular momentum. At an angle $\theta$ from the mean position, the restoring angular momentum is
$$
M = mgl \sin{\theta}
$$

We know that,
$$
\text{Torque} = \text{moment of inertial} \times \text{angular acceleration}
$$
$$
-mgl\sin\theta = I_A \alpha
$$
Where, $I_A$ is the moment of inertial relative to axis of rotation and $\alpha$ is the angular acceleration. In a compound pendulum, there are two axes. One is point of suspension and another is center of gravity of a pendulum. So from parallel axis theorem, we can write
$$
I_A = I_G + ml^2
$$
$$
I_G = mk^2
$$
where $k$ is the radius of gyration

For small amplitude $\theta$, $\sin{\theta} \approx \theta$
$$
\therefore \alpha = \frac{-mgl \theta}{I_A}
$$
$$
-\omega^2 \theta = \frac{-mgl \theta}{I_A}
$$
$$
\omega = \sqrt{\frac{mgl}{I_A}} = \sqrt{\frac{gl}{k^2 + l^2}}
$$
$$
T = \frac{2 \pi}{\omega} = 2 \pi \sqrt{\frac{k^2 + l^2}{gl}}
$$
$$
l^2 - \frac{T^2g}{4\pi ^2}l + k^2 = 0
$$
If $l_1$ and $l_2$ are the distances i.e. root of above equation for the same time period, $T$, then we can write
$$
l_1 + l_2 = \frac{T_2g}{4 \pi ^2} 
$$
and
$$
l_1l_2 = k^2
$$
From the 2nd above equation,
$$
g = \frac{4 \pi^2(l_1 + l_2)}{T^2}
$$
By measuring the values of $L = (l_1 + l_2)$ and $T$ graphically, g may be calculated from the above equation.


## Determination of the Radius of Curvature of a Plano Convex Lens by Newton's Ring Method

When a parallel beam of monochromatic light of wavelength $\lambda$ is incident on a combination of $P$ and $C$ as shown in Figure 1, a part of it is reflected from the lower surface of the lens, and a part after refraction through the film between $P$ and $C$ is reflected back from glass plate $P$. When the refracted ray strikes the glass plate, it undergoes a phase change of $180 \degree$ on reflection. After reflection, the two rays of $1$ and $2$ are obtained. These rays interfere each other producing alternate bright and dark rings around the point of contact. At the point of contact, the thickness of air film is zero. As a $\frac{\lambda}2$ path difference occurs, they cancel each other and a dark ring is obtained at the center. These rings were first discovered by Newton, that's why they are called Newton's rings

![[Pasted image 20250822014537.png]]
Let $D_n$ and $D_{n + p}$ be the diameters of the $n^{th}$ and $(n + p)^{th}$ bright or dark rings for normal incidence

Diameter of the $n^{th}$ dark ring is
$$
D_n = \sqrt{4n \lambda R}
$$
And diameter of the $(n + p)^{th}$ dark ring is
$$
D_{n + p} = \sqrt{4(n + p)\lambda R}
$$
By substracting the equation for the wavelength $\lambda$ of the incident monochromatic light is given by the equation
$$
R = \frac{D^2_{n + p} - D^2_n}{4p\lambda}
$$
where $R$ is the radius of curvature of plano-convex lens. Then by measuring the diameter $D_n$ and $D_{n + p}$ of the $n^{th}$ and $(n + p)^{th}$ bright or dark rings, the value of $\lambda$ can be determined

## Determination of the Wavelength of Sodium Light by a Plane Diffraction Grating with the Help of a Spectrometer

![[Pasted image 20250822033129.png]]

A parallel beam of light of wavelength, $\lambda$ coming out from a collimator falls normally on a plane diffraction grating. The grating is placed vertically on a prism table, a series of diffracted images of the collimator slit will be observed on both sides of the direct image.

The equation of interference maxima (that is the grating equation) can be written as,
$$
(a + b)\sin\theta = n\lambda
$$
Here, $\theta$ = angle of deviation; $(a + b)$ = grating element, and $n$ = order of number

Since $(a + b) = \frac1N$, where $N$ is the grating constant i.e. number of lines or ruling or grooves or scratches per cm of the grating surface
$$
\sin \theta = nN\lambda
$$
$$
\lambda = \frac{\sin \theta}{n N}
$$
The wavelength $\lambda$ can be determined from the Equation by the known values of $N$, the order($n$) and the diffraction angle ($\theta$)

When a current is passed through the filament it becomes hot, and the electrons gain enough energy to escape the filament. This process is called thermionic emission.

When a charged particle moves through a region in which both electric and magnetic fields are present, the resultant electromagnetic force experienced by the particle is given by the Lorentz force law,
$$
\overrightarrow{F} = q(\overrightarrow{E} + \overrightarrow{v} \times \overrightarrow B)
$$
where,
	$q$ is the charge of the particle
	$\overrightarrow v$ is the velocity of the particle
	$\overrightarrow E$ is the electric field intensity
	and $\overrightarrow B$ is the strength of the magnetic field

![[Pasted image 20250822033108.png]]

If the charged particle is an electron of charge $e$ and moves in a direction inclined at an angle $\theta$ to the direction of the magnetic field, then in the absence of electric field,
$$
\overrightarrow F = e(\overrightarrow v \times \overrightarrow B)
$$
The magnitude of the force is,
$$
F = evB\sin\theta
$$
If, $\theta \le 90 \degree$, the electron will move in a helical path
If, $\theta = 90 \degree$, we get
$$
F = evB
$$
The force is maximum and acts in a direction perpendicular to both the magnetic field and the direction of motion of the electron. As a result, the electron, the electron moves in a circular path with a constant speed $v$. The magnitude of the centripetal force, which arises due to the circular motion of the electro, will be equal to $evB$. If $m$ is the mass of the electron and $r$ is the radius of its circular math, then
$$
\frac{mv^2}r = evB
$$
$$
v = \frac{eBr}m
$$
If the electron is initially accelerated by an applied potential difference $V_a$, then we have
$$
eV_a = \frac 12 mv^2
$$
Substituting $v = \frac{eBr}m$ we get,
$$
\frac em = \frac{2V_a}{B^2r^2}
$$
The magnetic field is set up by a pair of Helmholtz coils which produces a fairly uniform magnetic field near its center. The value of $B$ in this region is given by
$$
B = \mu_0 \left( \frac 45 \right)^{3/2} \frac{ni}a = 8.99 \times 10^{-7} \frac{ni}a
$$
where,
	$n$ = number of turns in each of the pair of coils
	$i$ = current in the coil
	$a$ = radius of the coil
	$\mu_0$ = permeability of the empty space

The value of $e / m$ can e obtained from 2nd above equation.


## Determination of Plank's Constant

![[Pasted image 20250822034031.png]]

When electromagnetic radiations such as lights fall on a metallic surface, electrons are emitted from the surface. This phenomenon is known as photo-electric effect.

In 1905, Albert Einstein was able to provide an explanation of the photoelectric effect. He proposed that light acts like a particle having energy equal to $hv$, where, $h$ is Planck's constant and $v$ is the frequency of the incident light. These particles of light, called photons or quanta collide with and transfer energy to the electrons in the metal. The emitted electrons then use part of this energy to migrate to the surface of the metal and part of free themselves from the electrostatic attraction of the metallic surface; the remainder goes into the kinetic energy of the electrons. For those electrons at the surface of the metal, no energy is used by the electrons to migrate to the surface, and thus, these electrons end up with maximum possible kinetic energy, $K_{max}$. This can be expressed mathematically as,
$$
hv = \phi + K_{max}
$$
Where, $\phi$ is the work function of the material. i.e., the energy required to dislodge the electron from the surface.

Not all light will cause electron emission. If the frequency of the light is less than a threshold frequency, $f_0$, then the photons do not provide enough energy to overcome the work function of the surface. At frequencies above the threshold frequency, electrons will be emitted. Therefore, the work function express as,
$$
\phi = hv_0
$$
$$
hv = hv_0 + K_{max}
$$
A phototube consists of two electrodes in an evacuated glass tube. One electrode has a large photosensitive surface and is called the cathode or emitter. The other electrode is in the form of a wire and is the anode or the collector. I normal operation the node is held at a positive potential with respect to the cathode. When the cathode is exposed to light, electrons are emitted from its photosensitive surface. These electrons are attracted to the positive anode and form a current that can be measured with an ammeter or galvanometer. The kinetic energy of the emitted electrons is dependent upon the frequency of light striking the phototube, and the quantity of emitted electrons is dependent on the intensity of the light.

Because the work functions $w_0$ is constant for a material, the maximum kinetic energy depends directly on the frequency of the incident light. If the potential applied to the anode is gradually decreased to zero and then made negative then a certain voltage will be reached where the electrons emitted from the cathode will not have enough to reach or cross the anode. At this point kinetic energy of the electrons will totally convert into potential energy. At this voltage called "stopped potential", $V_s$, the electro current from the cathode to the anode will cease to flow
Therefore,
$$
E_k = E_{pot}
$$
Potential energy is equal to the amount of work that the electric field did to stop those electros they had applied force over distance to do some work.
$$
E_k = \text{work} = qU = eV_s
$$
where,
$e$ = the electronic charge and $V_s$ = the stopping potential
Therefore,
$$
K_{max} = eV_s
$$

## Determination of the Refractive Index of the Material of  A Prism by A Spectrometer

**Theory**:
Let a ray $PQ$ be the incident on the first face $AB$ of a prism and then after passing through the prism, finally emerges out through the outer face $AC$ in the direction $RS$. Let $i_1$ and $r_1$ be the respective angles of incidence and refraction at the first face of the prism and $r_2$ and $i_2$ be the corresponding quantities for the second face. Now the deviation of the ray is given by the angle $\angle{SOT}$,
$$
\delta = (i_1 - r_1) + (i_2 - r_2)
$$
But in the minimum deviation position, the ray passes symmetrically through the prism so that $i_1 = i_2$ and $r_1 = r_2$. Therefore the angle of the minimum deviation.
$$
\delta_m = 2(i_1 - r_1)
$$
But
$$
\angle LMR = r_1 + r_2 = 2r_1 = A
$$
$$
r_1 = \frac A2
$$
where $A$ = angle of prism
From the above equations we get
$$
i_1 = \frac{A + \delta _m}2
$$
Hence the refractive index of the material of the prism (for a particular color) is given by
$$
\mu = \frac{\sin{\frac{A + \delta _m}{2}}}{\sin\frac A2}
$$

## Determination of the Frequency of A Tuning Fork by Melde's Apparatus

Let one end of the string is attached to one prong of the fork $F$, and other end passes over a small pulley and is attached to a scale pan $S$. The string will be set into vibration by setting the tuning fork into vibration. As a result waves will proceed along the length of the string and will be reflected back on reaching the fixed end of the string. The superposition of the direct and reflected waves will form stationary waves, in which the extreme fixed ends of the string will always be nodes and in between them there may be one or more antinodes depending on the tension to which the string is subjected or the length of the string and the plane of vibration either perpendicular or parallel to the length of the string. The vibration may be in two different arrangements: transverse arrangements and longitudinal arrangement.
![[Pasted image 20250822230756.png]]

In transverse arrangement, the plane of vibration of the fork perpendicular to the length of the string, and the frequency of the fork ($N$) is equal to the frequency of the string ($n$). And in longitudinal arrangement, the plane of vibration of the fork parallel to the length of the string, and its frequency $N$ is equal to twice that of the string i.e. $N = 2n$
For the transverse arrangement,
$$
N = n = \frac 1 \lambda \sqrt{\frac TM}
$$
and for the longitudinal arrangement,
$$
N = 2n = \frac 2 \lambda \sqrt{\frac TM}
$$
where $T$ is the tension, $M$ is the mass per unit length and $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the stationary wave set up in the string.

The tension $T$ is given by
$$
T = (w + w_1)g
$$
where $w$ and $w_1$ are, respectively, the mass of the scale pan and mass on the scale pan

## Determination of The Specific Rotation of Sugar Solution by Means of Polarimeter

![[Pasted image 20250822232509.png]]
The specific rotation of a solution is a characteristic physical property of a given optically active molecules. If the specific rotation of a compound is positive, the compound is said to be dextrorotatory, if negative, it is referred to as levorotatory. The substances that rotate the plane of vibration to the right are called right-handed or dextrorotatory and the substances that rotate the plane of vibration to the left are called left-handed or levorotatory. The angle of rotation produced to the plane of vibration by an optically active substance in solution is proportional to
1. the thickness of the solution
2. the concentration of the solution or the density of the active substance in the solvent and
3. the nature of the substance

Thus we can write
$$
\theta \propto lc
$$
or,
$$
\theta = Slc
$$
or,
$$
S = \frac \theta{lc}
$$
where, $\theta$ is the angle of rotation produced, $l$ is the length of the solution in decimeters, $c$ is the concentration of the solution and $C = \frac mV gm/cm^3$, where $m$ is the mass of sugar in grams dissolved in water, $V$ is the volume of sugar solution, $S$ is a constant called specific rotation and depends upon the nature of the solution,
If $l$ is in decimeter then, $S = \frac{\theta}{lc}$

Polarimeter, balance, measuring cylinder, sugar, beaker, thermometer and a source of light.
